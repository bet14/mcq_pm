⚙ 5 Process Groups
The lifecycle phases that organize all 49 project management processes.
1
Initiating
Phase 1
Defines and authorizes the project or a new phase. The vision is captured, stakeholders are identified, and the project officially begins with formal authority granted to the PM.

Key Activities

  • Develop Project Charter
  • Identify Stakeholders
  • Define high-level scope
  • Assign Project Manager

Deliverables

  • Project Charter
  • Stakeholder Register
  • Business Case
  • Feasibility Study

Lifecycle Role

  • Marks the official start
  • Secures executive buy-in
  • Sets project boundaries
  • Enables formal authority
Example: A company wants to build an e-commerce platform. Initiating produces a Project Charter authorizing the project, naming a PM, and listing key stakeholders: CTO, marketing team, and end users.
2
Planning
Phase 2
Establishes the total scope, refines objectives, and develops the course of action. The most process-rich group in PMBOK with 24 processes — the blueprint for everything that follows.

Key Activities

  • Develop Project Management Plan
  • Define and decompose scope (WBS)
  • Estimate durations and costs
  • Plan risk, quality, resources

Deliverables

  • Project Management Plan
  • Work Breakdown Structure
  • Schedule Baseline
  • Cost Baseline / Budget

Lifecycle Role

  • Blueprint for execution
  • Prevents scope creep
  • Aligns team expectations
  • Enables change control
Example: The PM creates a WBS breaking the project into modules (frontend, backend, payments), estimates 6 months and $500K, identifies risks like API failures, and documents everything in a Project Management Plan.
3
Executing
Phase 3
Carries out the work defined in the project management plan. This is where the majority of the budget is consumed and actual deliverables are produced by the team.

Key Activities

  • Direct and Manage Project Work
  • Acquire and develop team
  • Manage communications
  • Conduct procurements

Deliverables

  • Work Products / Deliverables
  • Issue Log
  • Change Requests
  • Team Performance Assessments

Lifecycle Role

  • Produces actual outputs
  • Consumes most resources
  • Requires constant coordination
  • Feeds M&C with data
Example: Developers code the platform, designers build the UI, the PM holds daily standups, vendors are contracted for payment gateway integration, and progress is documented in status reports.
4
Monitoring & Controlling
Ongoing
Tracks, reviews, and regulates project progress and performance. Runs parallel to Executing — identifies deviations from the plan and initiates corrective or preventive actions.

Key Activities

  • Monitor performance (EVM)
  • Perform integrated change control
  • Validate and control scope
  • Control schedule and costs

Deliverables

  • Performance Reports
  • Change Log
  • Forecasts (EAC, ETC)
  • Variance Analysis

Lifecycle Role

  • Runs parallel to Executing
  • Ensures plan adherence
  • Triggers corrective actions
  • Manages change requests
Example: The PM notices the backend is 2 weeks behind using Earned Value Analysis. A change request is raised, the schedule is revised, and adding a developer is approved through the Change Control Board.
5
Closing
Phase 5
Finalizes all activities to formally complete the project or phase. Obtains formal acceptance, archives documents, releases resources, and captures lessons learned for future projects.

Key Activities

  • Obtain final acceptance
  • Close procurements
  • Archive project documents
  • Release project resources

Deliverables

  • Final Project Report
  • Lessons Learned Register
  • Closed Contracts
  • Formal Acceptance Sign-off

Lifecycle Role

  • Marks official completion
  • Transfers deliverables
  • Captures org knowledge
  • Releases team and budget
Example: The platform launches and is accepted by the client. The PM holds a retrospective, documents lessons learned (e.g., "API integration took 3x longer than estimated"), archives all documents, and formally closes vendor contracts.
📚 10 Knowledge Areas
Specialized domains of project management expertise applied across the process groups.
🔗
Knowledge Area 01
Project Integration Management
Coordinates all project management processes and activities into a cohesive whole — the glue that holds everything together.
Why It Matters
Ensures decisions in one area (e.g., scope change) are reflected across schedule, cost, and risk. The only KA present in ALL 5 process groups.
Key Processes
Develop CharterDevelop PM PlanDirect & Manage WorkIntegrated Change ControlClose Project
Real-World Example
A scope change to add a new feature is evaluated for its impact on schedule (+2 weeks), cost (+$20K), and risk (new API). The PM integrates all this before approving.
📐
Knowledge Area 02
Project Scope Management
Ensures the project includes all the work required — and only the work required — to complete the project successfully.
Why It Matters
Scope creep is one of the top reasons projects fail. Clear scope boundaries prevent unauthorized additions that blow up budget and schedule.
Key Processes
Collect RequirementsDefine ScopeCreate WBSValidate ScopeControl Scope
Real-World Example
A client asks to add a chat feature mid-project. Scope Management ensures this is formally evaluated and either approved with a schedule/cost adjustment or rejected.
📅
Knowledge Area 03
Project Schedule Management
Manages the timely completion of the project by defining activities, sequencing them, estimating durations, and controlling the schedule.
Why It Matters
Delays cascade — a late backend delays frontend testing, which delays UAT, which delays launch. Schedule management prevents this domino effect.
Key Processes
Define ActivitiesSequence ActivitiesEstimate DurationsDevelop ScheduleControl Schedule
Real-World Example
Using CPM (Critical Path Method), the PM identifies that database setup is on the critical path and allocates extra resources to protect it from delays.
💰
Knowledge Area 04
Project Cost Management
Involves planning, estimating, budgeting, and controlling costs so the project can be completed within the approved budget.
Why It Matters
Budget overruns can kill projects. Earned Value Management (EVM) provides early warning signals before overruns become catastrophic.
Key Processes
Estimate CostsDetermine BudgetControl Costs (EVM)
Real-World Example
EVM shows a CPI of 0.85 — meaning $1 spent yields only $0.85 of value. The PM forecasts a $60K overrun and takes corrective action immediately.
⭐
Knowledge Area 05
Project Quality Management
Ensures the project satisfies the needs for which it was undertaken by incorporating quality planning, assurance, and control.
Why It Matters
Delivering on time and on budget means nothing if the product does not work. Quality management ensures deliverables meet defined standards.
Key Processes
Plan Quality ManagementManage QualityControl Quality
Real-World Example
The team defines the website must load in under 2 seconds and have 99.9% uptime. QA engineers run performance tests and flag any page that fails the threshold.
👥
Knowledge Area 06
Project Resource Management
Identifies, acquires, and manages the resources needed — people, equipment, materials — for successful project completion.
Why It Matters
Projects are executed by people. Poor resource management leads to burnout, skill gaps, and missed deadlines. Effective management maximizes team performance.
Key Processes
Estimate Activity ResourcesAcquire ResourcesDevelop TeamManage TeamControl Resources
Real-World Example
The PM creates a RACI matrix, identifies that 2 senior developers are needed but only 1 is available internally, and contracts a freelancer for the gap.
📡
Knowledge Area 07
Project Communications Management
Ensures timely and appropriate planning, collection, creation, distribution, storage, and management of project information.
Why It Matters
Studies show 90% of a PM's time is spent communicating. Miscommunication is a leading cause of project failure. Right info, right people, right time.
Key Processes
Plan Communications ManagementManage CommunicationsMonitor Communications
Real-World Example
The PM creates a Communications Plan: executives get weekly status emails, the dev team has daily standups, and the client receives bi-weekly demo calls.
⚠
Knowledge Area 08
Project Risk Management
Identifies, analyzes, and responds to project risks — both threats (negative) and opportunities (positive) — to maximize project success probability.
Why It Matters
Every project has uncertainty. Proactive risk management prevents surprises, reduces the impact of negative events, and capitalizes on opportunities.
Key Processes
Identify RisksQualitative AnalysisQuantitative AnalysisPlan Risk ResponsesMonitor Risks
Real-World Example
The team identifies "third-party payment API may be deprecated" as a High/High risk. The response: prototype an alternative API in Sprint 2 as a contingency plan.
🛒
Knowledge Area 09
Project Procurement Management
Manages the processes of purchasing or acquiring products, services, or results needed from outside the project team.
Why It Matters
Most projects rely on external vendors. Poor procurement management leads to contract disputes, cost overruns, and delivery failures.
Key Processes
Plan Procurement ManagementConduct ProcurementsControl Procurements
Real-World Example
The PM issues an RFP for cloud hosting, evaluates bids from AWS, Azure, and GCP, selects AWS based on cost and SLA, and manages the contract throughout the project.
🤝
Knowledge Area 10
Project Stakeholder Management
Identifies people, groups, or organizations that could impact or be impacted by the project, and develops strategies to engage them effectively.
Why It Matters
A technically perfect project can still fail if key stakeholders are disengaged or resistant. Stakeholder management builds support and manages expectations proactively.
Key Processes
Identify StakeholdersPlan Stakeholder EngagementManage Stakeholder EngagementMonitor Engagement
Real-World Example
The PM maps stakeholders on a Power/Interest grid. The CFO (high power, low interest) gets concise financial summaries. End-users (low power, high interest) get frequent demos.
